# AI Coding Assistants, Developer Productivity, Economic Value, and Code Quality (2025–2026)

## Executive Summary

Adoption of AI coding assistants is now near-universal, but rigorous evidence on whether they improve real-world outcomes remains genuinely unresolved at every level examined — individual productivity, organizational ROI, and code-quality/security. The most rigorous individual-level randomized controlled trial found experienced developers were 19% slower with AI despite believing they were faster, while other large-sample studies report double-digit productivity gains, and the two literatures have not been reconciled. Economic-value evidence splits almost perfectly along vendor-interest lines: vendor-commissioned studies report ROI above 300%, while independent studies report that only a small fraction of enterprise AI initiatives ever deliver measurable, scaled financial return. Across code-quality and security studies, there is high-confidence, qualitative convergence that AI-generated code carries elevated defect and vulnerability rates, even though the specific magnitudes reported vary by an order of magnitude and no study has yet benchmarked new AI-native review/security tools against the traditional SAST tooling they are meant to supplement or replace. Overall, confidence is high that a perception-reality gap exists and that AI code carries elevated risk, but low-to-medium for any single directional verdict on productivity or ROI, and the list of open empirical gaps — especially around experience-level effects — has expanded rather than closed.

## Findings

- In a randomized controlled trial of 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 real issues using Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet, developers were 19% slower with AI assistance despite forecasting a 24% speed-up beforehand and self-reporting a 20% speed-up afterward; only 44% of AI suggestions were accepted. [1]
- In Microsoft's internal rollout, developers who adopted Claude Code/Copilot CLI merged approximately 24% more pull requests, sustained over four months, though the study's authors caution this measures output volume rather than proven value delivery. [17]
- A 2026 METR follow-up using newer agentic CLI tools was compromised by selection effects, a pay-rate change, and unreliable time-tracking, and is self-described as producing only 'weak evidence.' [16]
- In a large randomized controlled trial of 4,867 developers across three companies, developers with GitHub Copilot access completed 26.08% more tasks than those without. [2]
- Productivity effects from AI coding tools vary by developer experience level, per a workplace study of GenAI use. [3]
- In the DORA 2025 State of AI-Assisted Software Development survey, 90% of respondents reported AI adoption, alongside a 9% increase in bug rate, 91% more code-review time, and 154% larger pull requests — even as 59% reported positive quality perception and over 80% reported positive productivity perception. [4]
- In the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools, and 51% of professional developers use them daily. [5]
- Secondary aggregated reporting attributed to the Peng et al. dataset suggests junior/less-experienced developers gain 27-39% in productivity versus 8-13% for senior developers, with higher adoption rates among juniors; this specific breakdown is not independently verified against the primary paper and should be treated as low-confidence. [33, 34]
- A correlational analysis of roughly 800 developers found a 41% increase in bug rate for teams with Copilot access and no significant improvement in cycle time or PR throughput; this analysis did not break results down by seniority. [35]
- A large-scale analysis covering more than 135,000 developers across 425 organizations found that while junior developers adopt AI with greater frequency and intensity, the most senior tenure band shows the largest amount of 'time regained' among daily users — a finding directly at odds with claims that juniors benefit more. [36]
- Individual-level productivity gains (21% more tasks completed, 98% more PRs merged) did not reliably translate into organization-level delivery-performance improvement in the DORA 2025 data; AI appeared to amplify existing team dysfunction or strength rather than uniformly improve outcomes. [4]
- A survey of 415 practitioners using the SPACE framework found that frequent GenAI users report faster completion and higher output, but this may be offset by increased code-review burden and cognitive load from verifying AI output, suggesting perceived productivity gains 'may be spurious.' [37]
- A meta-analysis of 23 studies (27 effect sizes, spanning 2019-2025) concluded that 'when and where GenAI tools lead to productivity gains' remains an open question, with mixed effects on skill development. [38]
- An exploratory 'Truck Factor' study suggests that GenAI-driven code contribution may erode the traditional expertise and code-ownership signals historically used to define 'experience level,' complicating any experience-based analysis. [39]
- A Forrester Total Economic Impact study of GitHub Enterprise Cloud, commissioned by GitHub/Microsoft and based on a composite 5,000-developer organization built from five interviews at four organizations, projected $85.9M in benefits versus $18.1M in costs over three years, a $67.9M net present value, 376% ROI, and payback in under six months; this figure covers a bundle of Copilot, Actions, and Advanced Security rather than standalone Copilot ROI. [40]
- GitHub's own internal research reported 55% faster task completion, a 75% reduction in PR cycle time (9.6 to 2.4 days), Copilot authorship of roughly 46% of code in Copilot-enabled files, a ~30% suggestion acceptance rate, and 88% retention of AI-generated code in final submissions; the underlying methodology was not fully disclosed. [41]
- An independent MIT Media Lab/NANDA study, based on 52 executive interviews, a 153-leader survey, and analysis of 300 deployments, found that despite $30-40B in enterprise GenAI investment, 95% of pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact and only 5% scaled to create significant value; this study covers generative AI broadly, not coding assistants specifically. [42]
- An IBM Institute for Business Value global C-suite study found only 25% of AI initiatives deliver expected ROI, only 16% scale enterprise-wide, and while 79% of respondents report productivity gains, only 29% can confidently measure ROI; top-performing product-development teams reported a median GenAI ROI of 55%, and 85% of CEOs expect positive ROI on scaled investments by 2027. [43]
- Gartner projects that 75% of enterprise software engineers will use AI code assistants by 2028, and reports the AI code-assistant market grew from roughly $3.0-3.5B in early 2025 to $9.8-11.0B annualized by April 2026; separately, Gartner predicts more than 40% of broader agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027. [44]
- McKinsey's 2025 analysis found approximately 46% time savings on routine coding tasks compared with under 10% on complex work, and found that 57% of top-performing organizations invested in hands-on AI coaching versus 20% of bottom-performing organizations. [45]
- In a vendor-run benchmark of 50 real-world pull requests with seeded bugs, Greptile achieved an 82% catch rate compared with 58% for Cursor Bugbot, roughly mid-50s for GitHub Copilot, 44% for CodeRabbit, and 6% for Graphite; no traditional SAST tool was included as a baseline, and CodeRabbit separately publishes its own competing defect-rate report, raising a conflict-of-interest concern. [46]
- Aggregated comparative reviews indicate Greptile catches more bugs than competitors but produces more false positives (roughly 11) compared with CodeRabbit (roughly 2); CodeRabbit layers AI on top of more than 40 bundled linters and SAST scanners, while Qodo's multi-agent design is noted for accuracy plus test generation. [47]
- CodeRabbit's 'State of AI vs. Human Code Generation' report, based on 470 real GitHub pull requests using Poisson rate-ratio analysis with 95% confidence intervals, found AI-authored PRs had approximately 1.7 times more issues than human-authored PRs, elevated across logic, maintainability, security, and performance categories. [19]
- Veracode's GenAI Code Security Report (2025, with a Spring 2026 update), testing more than 80 tasks across 100+ LLMs with Veracode's own SAST tooling, found 45% of AI-generated samples introduced an OWASP Top 10 vulnerability, with Java the worst-performing language (72% failure), cross-site-scripting defense failing 86% of the time, log-injection defense failing 88% of the time, and no improvement in security from newer or larger models. [22]
- GitGuardian's 'State of Secrets Sprawl 2026' telemetry report found AI-assisted commits leak secrets at a 3.2% rate — roughly twice the non-AI baseline — with 28.65 million new secrets found on public GitHub in 2025 (a 34% year-over-year increase), an 81% surge in AI-service-credential leaks, and 64% of valid 2022-era secrets still unrevoked as of 2026. [27]
- A peer-reviewed May 2026 study (EASE 2026) using GitHub's 'AIDev' dataset found most AI-generated pull requests receive no review at all, and when reviewed, review activity is dominated by AI agents rather than humans, whereas human-authored PRs are more likely to receive human-only review and direct feedback. [48]
- Secondary roundups attribute to an unverified InfoWorld study the claims that Semgrep alone achieves 35.7% precision, GPT-4 alone achieves 65.5% precision, and a hybrid SAST+LLM pipeline achieves 89.5% precision; separately, these roundups claim AI code-review tools detect 42-85% of runtime bugs versus under 20% for traditional static analysis alone. These figures are explicitly flagged as unverified, with no primary source located. [49]
- Analysis of 304,362 AI-authored commits across 6,275 repositories found unresolved AI-introduced technical-debt issues grew from a few hundred to more than 110,000 surviving issues in roughly one year. [15]
- Analysis of 623 million lines of code from 2023-2026 found task-completion productivity up 21%, code duplication up 81% since 2023, and refactoring activity falling to 3.8% of changes from 21% in 2022. [28]
- A difference-in-differences causal study found static-analysis warnings up 18% and cognitive complexity up 39% in AI-adopting repositories compared with non-adopting repositories, with initial velocity gains fading over time while quality degradation persisted. [30]
- A peer-reviewed study (Empirical Software Engineering, 2026) with 151 participants found no systematic maintainability advantage or disadvantage when other developers maintained AI-co-developed code, alongside a 30.7% median completion-time reduction in initial development — directly contradicting findings of degradation elsewhere in the literature. [32]
- A baseline study found approximately 40% of GitHub Copilot suggestions contained vulnerabilities. [21]
- An outcome-defect-classification (ODC) study of more than 500,000 samples examined structural characteristics of AI-generated code defects. [23]
- A systematic review of 19 studies examined AI-generated code security across the literature. [24]
- A recurring perception-reality gap, where developers and organizations report positive sentiment about AI-assisted coding despite measured negative or mixed quality/security outcomes, appears across four or more independent methodologies. [1, 4, 10]

## Conflicting Evidence

- Faster vs. slower: Individual RCT evidence (METR: 19% slower) directly conflicts with other productivity studies reporting gains (Peng et al.: 26% more tasks completed; Microsoft: 24% more PRs merged), and with mixed evidence from newer agentic-tool follow-ups. Unresolved. [1,2,3,16,17]
- Microsoft's output-volume gain vs. METR's slowdown: a 24% increase in merged PRs is not reconciled with a 19% slowdown finding from a more tightly controlled RCT; different populations and metrics may explain the gap, but no source tests this directly. [1,17]
- DORA's internal contradiction: positive quality and productivity perceptions (59%/80%+) sit alongside DORA's own measured 9% bug-rate increase and 91% more code-review time; a parallel, unresolved disconnect exists between individual-level gains and organization-level delivery performance within the same report. [4]
- Junior-gains-more vs. senior-regains-more-time: secondary-sourced claims that juniors gain 3-4x more than seniors conflict with GetDX's larger-sample finding that senior daily users regain the most time. Neither position is resolved as correct; different populations and constructs are measured. [33,34,36]
- METR's senior slowdown vs. Peng et al.'s aggregate senior gain: METR's experienced-developer slowdown is not reconciled with secondary reports of an 8-13% senior productivity gain derived from Peng et al.; population, methodology, and familiarity confounds remain untested. [1,2,33,34]
- Three-way inconsistency: METR, the Peng-et-al.-derived junior/senior claim, and GetDX cannot all be simultaneously true of a single unified 'experience effect.' No source attempts reconciliation; flagged as the single most significant unresolved empirical question in the productivity literature. [1,2,33,34,36]
- Uplevel's null result vs. Peng et al.'s completed-task gain: partially reconcilable if increased review/rework burden absorbs completed-task gains (consistent with the Afroz et al. mechanism), but this reconciliation is unconfirmed. [2,35,37]
- Vendor-sourced high ROI vs. independent low-ROI-realization findings: Forrester's (GitHub-commissioned) 376% ROI and GitHub's internal productivity metrics stand in tension with MIT/NANDA's 95%-pilot-failure finding and IBM's 25%-ROI-realization figure. Not strictly comparable in scope (independent figures are not coding-assistant-specific), and vendor-commissioned figures also warrant low trust given tiny interview samples and undisclosed methodology. Whether the general enterprise-AI ROI pattern applies with equal force to coding assistants specifically remains untested. [40,41,42,43]
- Gartner's simultaneous growth and cancellation forecasts: different scopes (coding-assistant market growth vs. broader agentic-AI project cancellation), not a strict contradiction, but risk of conflation is flagged. [44]
- GitHub's uniform speed gains vs. McKinsey/METR's complexity-dependent split: GitHub's blog reports broad, large speed gains, while McKinsey and METR find much smaller or negative gains on complex tasks. No source tests this directly. [41,45,1]
- Catch-rate/detection claims vs. underlying-defect-rate findings: Greptile's 82% catch rate and unverified 42-85% detection claims measure a different construct than CodeRabbit's and Veracode's defect-rate findings; framing tension rather than a strict numerical contradiction, but the two should not be conflated. [46,49,19,22]
- Vendor self-interest in benchmarking: CodeRabbit scored 44% in Greptile's own benchmark while separately publishing a competing defect-rate whitepaper showing AI code has 1.7x more issues than human code. No neutral arbiter exists for either figure. [46,19]
- Precision/recall tradeoff among AI-native tools: Greptile shows a higher catch rate but more false positives than CodeRabbit; sourced only from informal aggregated reviews, not resolved to a 'better tool' verdict. [47]
- Degradation vs. no degradation: production-repository trend studies showing rising technical debt, duplication, and complexity directly conflict with a peer-reviewed controlled study finding no systematic maintainability disadvantage from AI-co-developed code. Both positions carried forward as unresolved. [15,28,30,32]
- Security-defect magnitude: qualitative convergence exists that AI-generated code carries elevated vulnerability rates, but specific magnitudes reported (e.g., ~40% vulnerable suggestions vs. 45% OWASP Top 10 introduction rate vs. 1.7x issue ratio) diverge by an order of magnitude and remain unreconciled. [19,21,22,23,24]

## Confidence and Limitations

High confidence: near-universal adoption of AI coding tools [4,5]; a persistent perception-reality gap between reported sentiment and measured outcomes, reinforced across multiple methodologies [1,4,10,37]; AI-generated code carries materially higher defect/security-vulnerability rates in qualitative terms, though magnitudes diverge [19,21,22,23,24]; organizational throughput/output-volume metrics increase with AI adoption [4,17,19,28,2]. Medium-high confidence: productivity effects are real but task-, context-, and population-dependent [1,2,3,45,35]; individual-level gains do not reliably scale to organizational or economic outcomes, strengthened by triangulation across DORA, MIT/NANDA, and IBM data, though MIT/NANDA's applicability specifically to coding assistants remains formally unconfirmed [4,42,43]; existing review, CI, linting, and secret-scanning processes only partially mitigate AI-introduced defects [21,25,26], with newer evidence adding a distinct "lack of review coverage" mechanism alongside a heuristic-mismatch hypothesis [48]. Medium confidence: directional productivity effects for the newest agentic tools remain uncertain [16,17]; junior developers adopt AI with greater frequency and intensity than seniors, corroborated across independent sources despite disagreement on who benefits more [33,34,36]; AI-generated defects may be structurally distinct from human-introduced defects [23]; the finding that newer/larger models show no security improvement now has some temporal consistency across two report cycles but remains single-vendor sourced [22]; accumulating technical debt has quasi-causal support [15,28,30]. Low confidence: specific junior/senior productivity magnitude figures such as "3-4x" [33,34]; any reconciliation of experience-level effects with METR's findings — these are actively contradicted, not merely uncertain [1,2,33,34,36]; any ROI or economic-value figure for AI coding assistants specifically, in either direction [40,41,42,43,44,45]; vendor-sourced unverified magnitude figures generally [49]; AI-native code-review catch-rate rankings as a durable, generalizable verdict [46,47]; hybrid SAST+LLM precision figures, explicitly unverified with no located primary source [49]. Unsourced or excluded from this report: an unsourced 89.3% technical-debt figure and a 2.74x AI-vulnerability-multiplier figure attributed to a secondary citation were excluded entirely, as their primary sources could not be located. Scope limitations: no independent, large-sample, coding-assistant-specific, cost-inclusive ROI study exists anywhere in the reviewed literature; no study benchmarks AI-native review/security tools against named traditional SAST tools on a shared corpus with matched metrics; sample sizes across the detection-tooling literature remain modest (50-470 PRs), and several ROI-cluster studies (Gartner, IBM, McKinsey) do not disclose sample sizes at all. Methodological caveats: the METR 2026 follow-up is self-disqualified by its own authors [16]; the Microsoft PR-volume study is explicitly caveated as an output proxy [17]; the Forrester TEI ROI study is vendor-commissioned, based on five interviews at four organizations projected onto a hypothetical composite organization [40]; the MIT/NANDA and IBM ROI-realization figures address generative AI/enterprise AI broadly, not coding assistants specifically [42,43]; several vendor-sourced statistics (Qodo, Veracode, Greptile, CodeRabbit) lack independent verification and in some cases involve a vendor benchmarking its own product or a close competitor's [22,24,46,47]. This report was produced by the multi-agent research pipeline (search, analysis, synthesis, and report-writing stages), merging three new follow-up topics (experience-level productivity/bug rates, enterprise ROI, and AI-native vs. traditional detection rates) with the prior report's evidence base.

- Open gap: Isolating codebase familiarity as a variable independent of developer seniority
- Open gap: METR-style RCT design applied specifically to junior developers
- Open gap: Testing the verification-burden/cognitive-load mechanism against seniority or defect-rate data directly
- Open gap: Bug/defect rates stratified explicitly by developer seniority
- Open gap: Controlled before/after experiments isolating code-review/CI gate effectiveness against AI-generated code specifically
- Open gap: Cross-tool benchmark comparing AI-native review tools against named traditional SAST tools on a shared corpus
- Open gap: Empirical testing of whether CI/linting/secret-scanning catches AI-generated defects at different rates than human-authored defects of the same type
- Open gap: Comparative secrets-scanning detection capability across traditional vs. AI-native tools
- Open gap: Longitudinal, model-version-controlled study of whether detection-tool efficacy is improving or degrading as LLM code-generation capability advances
- Open gap: Verification of the 89.5% hybrid SAST+LLM precision claim against a primary source
- Open gap: Large-scale, cross-tool-category corpus for detection-tooling benchmarks (current studies use only 50-470 PRs)

- Open gap: Task-complexity breakdown of GitHub's blog metrics to check consistency with the McKinsey/METR routine-vs-complex split
- Open gap: Disclosed sample sizes for Gartner, IBM, and McKinsey coding-productivity/ROI studies
- Open gap: Sector, industry, or company-size breakdown of ROI realization
- Open gap: Independent RCT or controlled study at enterprise/organizational economic-value scale
- Open gap: Downstream-cost accounting (bugs, security, maintenance, technical debt) in ROI calculations
- Open gap: Organizational and process moderator analysis for technical-debt accumulation
- Open gap: Reconciliation of controlled-experiment (no maintainability degradation) vs. production-repo-trend (degradation) findings
- Open gap: Downstream cost quantification (incidents, dollars, delivery slowdown) of AI-related technical debt
- Open gap: Standardized LLM-specific technical-debt metrics
- Open gap: Multi-year longitudinal study combined with rigorous causal design for maintainability
