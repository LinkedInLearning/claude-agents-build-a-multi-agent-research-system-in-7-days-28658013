# Impact of AI Coding Assistants on Software Developer Productivity and Code Quality

## Executive Summary

The evidence on AI coding assistants is split along a consistent fault line: controlled, short-horizon studies on bounded tasks — many run or funded by AI-tool vendors — tend to show sizeable productivity and quality gains, while independent, longitudinal, telemetry-based studies on complex or legacy codebases tend to show null or negative effects. Reported productivity effects range from a 55.8% speedup (Peng et al.) to a 19% slowdown (METR) depending on task type and codebase familiarity. Code-quality and security findings show a similar split: a GitHub-run RCT reports quality gains, while independent telemetry (GitClear, Uplevel), a large-scale commit analysis, and Google's DORA program report rising code duplication, bug rates, technical debt, and declining delivery stability. A recurring "perception-reality gap" appears in both domains, with developers and organizations self-reporting confidence and gains that objective measurements do not corroborate or directly contradict. No source in the underlying research reconciles these divergences with a controlled, apples-to-apples comparison, so the overall confidence in any single generalizable effect size is low, even though confidence that AI assistants produce some measurable effect on both speed and code health is comparatively higher.

## Findings

- In a randomized controlled trial of 95 professional developers, those using an AI coding assistant completed a task 55.8% faster (71 minutes vs. 161 minutes; p=0.0017, 95% CI [21%, 89%]). [1]
- Across three field-experiment RCTs covering 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 company, Copilot access was associated with a 26.08% (SE 10.3%) increase in completed tasks. [2]
- In a Google Research RCT of 96 full-time engineers on an enterprise logging task, AI assistance produced roughly 21% faster completion (96 minutes vs. 114 minutes). [3]
- A GitHub/Accenture enterprise RCT and telemetry study found an 8.69% increase in pull requests per developer, a 15% increase in PR merge rate, an 84% increase in successful builds, a roughly 30% suggestion acceptance rate, and 60-75% of developers self-reporting greater fulfillment and satisfaction. [4]
- A reported ANZ Bank six-week experiment found an overall 42.36% faster completion time (beginners +52.27%, intermediate +41.6%, advanced +40.48%); this source is sourced only through secondary aggregators and its provenance is unverified. [5]
- A survey of 415 frequent GenAI users (Afroz et al., using the SPACE framework) found self-reported faster completion and higher output volume, but the same study found these perceived gains offset by increased code-review burden and cognitive load in verifying AI output, with the authors characterizing net perceived gains as potentially "spurious." [6]
- In a METR RCT of 16 experienced developers working on 246 real issues in codebases they knew well (~5 years average experience), AI assistance made task completion 19% slower, even though developers had predicted beforehand that AI would make them 24% faster and believed afterward that it had made them 20% faster. [7]
- A systematic review of 37-39 peer-reviewed studies from 2014-2024 found heterogeneous benefits and risks with no single unified effect size for productivity. [8]
- A longitudinal mixed-methods case study tracked pre/post-adoption productivity metrics and qualitative feedback but did not report a specific direction or effect size. [9]
- An empirical analysis of 733 Copilot-generated code snippets found security weaknesses in 29.5% of Python snippets and 24.2% of JavaScript snippets, spanning 43 CWE categories (including 8 in the 2023 CWE Top-25); combining Copilot Chat with static-analysis warnings fixed up to 55.5% of identified security issues, leaving 44.5% unresolved. [10]
- An analysis of 1,689 Copilot-generated programs ("Asleep at the Keyboard?," 2021) found approximately 40% contained vulnerabilities when benchmarked against the MITRE Top-25 (roughly 50% for C, roughly 39% for Python). [11]
- In a controlled user study, developers using an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than a control group, while being more confident that their code was secure. [12]
- A large-scale 2025 analysis of AI-associated code found vulnerability rates of 16.18-18.50% in Python, 8.66-8.99% in JavaScript, and 2.50-7.14% in TypeScript, with 87.9% of all AI-associated files found free of detectable CWEs. [13]
- GitClear's analysis of 211 million lines of code found duplicated/copy-pasted code increased 8x in 2024 (copy-paste exceeded refactored code for the first time), refactored code share fell from roughly 25% in 2021 to under 10% in 2024, and code churn rose from a roughly 3.3% baseline to 5.7% in 2024 and 7.1% in 2025. [14]
- Uplevel Data Labs found developers with Copilot access had a 41% higher bug rate, with no corresponding change in throughput or productivity. [15]
- A commit-level analysis ("Debt Behind the AI Boom") found unresolved technical debt in AI-authored commits grew from hundreds of issues in early 2025 to over 110,000 by February 2026 across 304,362 commits and 6,275 repositories, with a forward-looking projection of roughly 4x traditional maintenance costs by year two. [16]
- CodeRabbit's analysis of AI-assisted pull requests found recurring logic errors and correctness, readability, and security issues, along with added reviewer burden. [17]
- Google's DORA 2024/2025 reports found AI adoption correlates negatively with software delivery stability, and that developer trust in AI-generated code fell from 87.9% in 2024 to roughly 70% in 2025 even as adoption rose from 76% to 90%. [18]
- In an official GitHub RCT of 202 developers with 5+ years of experience, Copilot users were 53.2% more likely to pass all 10 unit tests on a task, and scored higher on readability (+3.62%), reliability (+2.94%), maintainability (+2.47%), and conciseness (+4.16%). [19]
- In the same DORA 2025 report, over 80% of respondents self-reported increased productivity and 59% self-reported increased code quality, figures that sit alongside the report's own negative delivery-stability correlation and declining trust metrics. [18]

## Conflicting Evidence

- Direction of the productivity effect: Peng et al., Cui et al., Google Research, the GitHub/Accenture study, and the ANZ Bank report all find speed gains ranging from roughly +21% to +56%, while METR's RCT finds a 19% slowdown. This is attributed to task type: short, bounded, greenfield tasks show gains, while complex, real-world maintenance work on familiar codebases (METR's design) shows a loss. Neither result generalizes to the other context; confidence is medium within each scope but low for any general claim.
- Experience level as a moderator: Cui et al. and the ANZ Bank report suggest junior developers benefit more from AI assistance, while Google Research finds seniors benefit more. This is unresolved in the underlying research; no quality or security study breaks out effects by experience level at scale.
- Code quality: improvement vs. degradation: GitHub's own RCT finds quality improvements (higher unit-test pass rates, readability, reliability, maintainability), while GitClear, Uplevel, CodeRabbit, and DORA independently report degradation (more duplication, higher bug rates, recurring logic/correctness issues, negative delivery-stability correlation). Multiple independent, converging sources favor real-world degradation; the GitHub RCT result is best read narrowly as valid for short, controlled tasks rather than as a general claim.
- DORA's internal tension: the same DORA report finds self-reported productivity and quality gains (80%+ and 59% respectively) coexisting with a negative correlation between AI adoption and delivery stability, and falling trust in AI-generated code (87.9% to roughly 70%). The behavioral/outcome metrics (delivery stability, trust) arguably deserve more weight than the self-reported perception metrics here.
- Magnitude of vulnerability rates: estimates of AI-generated code vulnerability rates diverge substantially: roughly 40% (Pearce et al., 2021), 24.2%-29.5% (2023 snippet analysis), and single digits to high teens by language with 87.9% of files clean overall (2025 large-scale analysis). Differing CWE taxonomies, sampling methods, and model versions across studies prevent full reconciliation; the most recent 2025 estimate is weighted as a probable declining trend, but this is not established with certainty.
- Master pattern (unresolved): vendor-affiliated or vendor-run RCTs report positive effects in both productivity and quality; independent longitudinal or telemetry-based studies report null or negative effects in both domains. No study directly compares vendor-run controlled conditions against independent real-world telemetry using a shared methodology, leaving this the most consequential unresolved question in the evidence base.

## Confidence and Limitations

Overall confidence is medium on productivity effects existing at all, but low on the sign or magnitude of that effect for any specific real-world task, given that the four independent RCTs (Peng et al., Cui et al., Google Research, METR) disagree on direction across different task contexts. Confidence is medium-to-high that AI-generated code carries elevated, often underestimated security/quality risk, based on convergence across academic code-sample studies, GitClear telemetry, Uplevel, CodeRabbit, and DORA, but low confidence in precise vulnerability-rate magnitudes, which vary roughly 5x-8x across studies. Several studies have small or unspecified sample sizes: Peng et al. (n=95, single task type), METR (n=16, narrow population/context), Google Research (n=96, single Google-specific bounded task), GitHub/Accenture (n unspecified, partly self-report), and Uplevel/CodeRabbit (sample sizes unspecified). The ANZ Bank study is sourced only through secondary aggregators with unverified provenance. An enterprise three-week RCT (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18334) is referenced as lacking specific quantitative outcomes in the available summary. The "Debt Behind the AI Boom" technical-debt cost projection (roughly 4x traditional maintenance costs by year two) is explicitly forward-looking rather than observed. No source reconciles why task complexity or codebase familiarity flips the productivity effect between studies. Long-term, multi-year, causal (rather than correlational/observational) studies of productivity and of quality/security are largely absent. No study simultaneously measures productivity and quality/security for the same developers, tasks, and timeframe, which would be needed to test whether productivity gains are "purchased" at the cost of code quality. No head-to-head cross-tool comparison exists for either productivity or quality, no study quantifies security or quality impact by developer experience level at scale, and no breakdown exists between newer/agentic AI tools and earlier Copilot-generation tools. Demographic, team-size, and industry breakdowns are limited across the productivity literature.
